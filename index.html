    Mixing and kneading

      The water temperature uses the bakers' desired-dough-temperature rule, with friction factors of about 6–8 °F by hand and 20–24 °F in a stand mixer (King Arthur Baking, America's Test Kitchen). Mixer makers advise only the lowest speeds with a dough hook (KitchenAid). Kneading times follow Breville and commercial mixing guides.

      How the yeast is worked out

      The calculator uses Craig's baker's yeast prediction model from the pizzamaking.com forum. Most serious pizza calculators are built on it. It has two parts.

      1. Temperature. Yeast speed follows the growth curve from Gänzle, Ehmann & Hammes (1998), a study of sourdough yeast, with Craig's fitted values: rate = a · x^b · e^(c·x), where x = 45 − T, a = 0.02646, b = 2.037 and c = −0.199. Compared with 21 °C, an hour at 4 °C counts 0.10 h, at 6 °C 0.13 h, at 18 °C 0.69 h, at 24 °C 1.37 h and at 30 °C 2.3 h. The curve peaks at about 35 °C.

      2. Amount. 0.048% instant yeast makes a dough ready in 12 hours at 21 °C (70 °F). For other times, yeast scales with (hours ÷ 12)^−1.6. With these settings the model reproduces Craig's chart for a 4 °C fridge: 0.63% for 24 h (chart 0.64%) and 0.21% for 48 h (chart 0.20%).

      The calculator follows the dough temperature minute by minute and adds up the hours. Dough does not get cold the moment it goes in the fridge. Pizzeria guidance is 1.5 to 3.5 hours to cool dough balls, depending on their size, so the model lets the dough temperature drift toward the fridge (or the room) with a 1¼-hour time constant.

      Two corrections

      Salt. Salt slows yeast a lot. Belz et al. (2017) measured gas in 3 hours: dough with about 2.9% salt (on flour) made 38% less gas than dough with 1.7%. The calculator takes Craig's dough as 2.8% salt and scales the yeast by the measured gas ratio. That gives × 0.72 for 2% salt, × 1.08 for 3% and × 1.29 for 3.5%. The study ran 3 hours at 30 °C, and in a long cold rise the effect may be smaller.

      Starting point. Many forum users find Craig's numbers on the low side, and published recipes use more. "Typical recipes" uses 1.5 × Craig, which lands in the middle of the published numbers below. "Craig's chart" uses the model unchanged.

      Yeast types are converted with the usual 1 : 0.4 : 0.33 ratio of fresh to active dry to instant, so active dry is 1.2 × instant and fresh is 3 × instant.

      Your fridge matters most. A 6 °C fridge ferments a third faster than a 4 °C one, so put a thermometer in it once. Then use Your adjustment: if the dough was slow, add 25 to 50%; if it was over-risen, take some off.
